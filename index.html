"I Would Launch a Nuke"

Guhan Nagarajan on Nuclear Control

Published Jan 8, 2026

I, Guhan Nagarajan, assert that if I were ever to come into possession of nuclear weapons—whether tactical or strategic—I would inevitably use them. This claim is not made lightly, nor is it intended as a declaration of recklessness, but rather as a hypothetical premise necessary for a moral evaluation of nuclear weapons possession. By assuming that my use of such weapons would be foreseeable, the ethical inquiry shifts away from abstract speculation and toward concrete responsibility. If possession predictably results in use, then the moral status of possession itself must be interrogated. Under this framework, my possession of nuclear weapons would constitute a profound moral wrong, regardless of whether the weapons were ever actually launched.

Nuclear weapons are uniquely destructive instruments. Unlike conventional arms, they possess the capacity for indiscriminate, large-scale devastation that cannot be meaningfully constrained by intention or precision. Their effects extend far beyond immediate military targets, inflicting catastrophic harm on civilian populations, ecosystems, and future generations through radiation, environmental damage, and long-term health consequences. Because these harms are not only massive but also irreversible, the moral threshold for justifying nuclear weapons use is extraordinarily high—arguably unattainable. Consequently, any ethical system that takes human life, proportionality, or responsibility seriously must regard nuclear weapons as fundamentally dangerous in a way that exceeds ordinary tools of war.

Given this reality, entrusting nuclear weapons to an individual who would foreseeably authorize their use is ethically indefensible. Moral responsibility does not begin at the moment a weapon is launched; it begins earlier, at the point where institutions or societies decide who may wield such power. If it is known—or reasonably expected—that a particular individual would use nuclear weapons if given access to them, then granting that access constitutes a failure of moral judgment. In this sense, the wrongdoing is not merely personal but systemic. Allowing possession under conditions of foreseeable misuse represents a conscious acceptance of catastrophic risk.

This argument aligns with broader principles in moral philosophy concerning foreseeability and negligence. Ethical accountability is not limited to intentional harm; it also includes outcomes that are reasonably predictable. If an authority knowingly places a devastating capability into the hands of someone likely to employ it, the authority shares moral responsibility for any resulting harm. Thus, even if the individual weapon-holder bears direct blame for launching the weapon, those who enabled that possession cannot escape ethical culpability. The possession itself becomes morally tainted because it creates an unacceptable risk of harm.

Furthermore, this reasoning challenges the common tendency to isolate immorality solely in the act of use. While launching a nuclear weapon is unquestionably immoral, focusing exclusively on that moment obscures the deeper ethical failure that precedes it. By the time a nuclear weapon is launched, the moral error has already occurred: the error of permitting such a weapon to exist under the control of someone likely to deploy it. From this perspective, the act of possession is not morally neutral. Instead, it is an active condition that makes mass destruction possible, and therefore morally significant in its own right.

The hypothetical case of my own possession underscores a broader critique of nuclear deterrence and individual authority over catastrophic force. Deterrence theory often assumes rational restraint, yet this assumption collapses when applied to individuals whose use of such weapons is foreseeable. If deterrence depends on self-control and restraint, then placing nuclear weapons in the hands of someone who lacks—or claims to lack—such restraint undermines the entire moral justification for their existence. In this sense, my hypothetical possession serves as a reductio ad absurdum, revealing the ethical fragility of nuclear weapons governance more generally.

Ultimately, the conclusion follows that my possession of nuclear weapons would be immoral, not merely because of what I might do with them, but because of the unacceptable risk my possession would create. Moral systems that prioritize the prevention of catastrophic harm cannot tolerate such risk, especially when the consequences involve mass death and irreversible suffering. Therefore, the immorality lies not only in the act of launching nuclear weapons, but in the prior decision to allow someone like Guhan Nagarajan to possess them at all. The very existence of such possession represents a moral failure, as it knowingly places humanity at risk of catastrophic harm.

Guhan Nagarajan is a high schooler at the Harker School in California. When he's not writing articles about nuclear bombs, he likes to launch them.
